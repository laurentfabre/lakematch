How good is Zingg + Jev, and what makes it drastically better

The entity-resolution stack planned for the lake (Zingg on Spark 4.1, with TypeSafe's Jev as labeller) was measured on public labelled corpora against the published state of the art, then audited by an independent model (Codex on gpt-6-astra), whose findings are folded in below. Under its default configuration Zingg recovered 75 % of the true links on 10 000 person records; a nearest-neighbour candidate step recovers all of them. Jev's labels, never wrong when confident, are what let a pipeline reject non-matches without any human label. Every experiment together cost about two dollars.

The complete system, end to end

FEBRL4: link 5 000 person records to their 5 000 corrupted duplicates. Zingg does everything it does in production; only the labeller changes. The harder task: half the partners removed

2 500 of the 5 000 records now have no match and must be rejected. No ground truth is used except to score. The decision threshold (0.5), the confidence level (0.9) and the arbitration bands are constants, and the rule "Jev overrides only when confident" was adopted after seeing test results on the easy task, so read the last two columns as tuned on this family of data.

Zingg rejects the unmatched records almost perfectly (precision 0.999 to 1.000) and again recovers about three quarters of the true links; trained on Jev's labels instead of perfect ones it scores 0.849 (P 1.000, R 0.738), so here too Jev's labels cost it nothing.

Here Jev earns its place. Nearest neighbour alone collapses to 0.667 because it cannot say no. Jev's labels (1 122 confident answers kept out of 1 200 asked in these three runs, none wrong) are what teach the pipeline to reject: a single similarity cut learnt from them is enough when the SSN is present, the classifier is needed once it is hidden (0.761 → 0.936), and Jev's arbitration of the unsure anchors adds another 0.2 to 2.8 points.

Under the tested configuration (default settings, 6 to 12 labelling rounds, exact match on postcode and state) Zingg recovered 75.1 % of the links: 32.0 % when the first names differed and 95.6 % when they were equal. The final links cannot tell a pair that was never compared from one the classifier rejected, so this shows where Zingg loses links, not why. Blocking is the likeliest cause (doubling the labels recovered 4 more links out of 1 245 missing) but no candidate-level evidence or configuration sweep confirms it. For contacts, where the same person is "Jean", "J." and "Dupont, Jean", a first-name-sensitive failure is the worst kind either way.

  1. Generate candidates by nearest neighbour, and measure their recall first. Top-k character-trigram TF-IDF over the concatenated match fields kept every true link within five candidates on FEBRL4, where Zingg under default settings recovered 75 %. Whether Zingg's loss is blocking or classification is not established; either way Zingg cannot accept outside candidate pairs, so this replaces its train and match steps.
  2. Use Jev's confident labels to learn when to say no. 2 173 confident labels across six FEBRL4 runs, none wrong, and no human label. On the task with unmatched records they lift nearest-neighbour matching from 0.667 to 0.94–0.999.
  3. Stack only where validation says it pays. Similarity features → gradient boosting → its score plus Jev's answers → logistic regression gave +10 points on Amazon-Google, a probable +1.7 on people, and nothing on the two electronics sets, where plain zero-shot Jev is the pick. Choose on validation data, never on test.
  4. Send Jev only the uncertain band when cost or latency matters: 19 to 32 % of pairs on the product sets, 1 to 11 % on FEBRL4.
  5. Let Jev label when the data is clean and judge when it is ambiguous. One number tells them apart: the share of pairs on which Jev is confident at 0.9 (about 90 % on FEBRL4, 9 % on BPID). Training a classifier on Jev's labels fails on ambiguous data (0.44 to 0.64).
  6. For people, test the attribute questions on your own labelled pairs before adopting them: +2.8 F1 on BPID for 25 % more tokens, but inside the noise. Demonstrations alone lowered the people score.
  7. For merchants and product-like strings, add about ten related demonstrations from pairs you have already labelled, and consider select-among-top-4 where near-duplicates compete.
  8. Override only on confidence. Jev replaces the classifier's decision when it is sure; otherwise the classifier stands. Dropping a link whenever Jev hesitated cost 52 true links.

Caveats

  • Published language-model numbers use the best of ten prompts chosen on the test set and downsampled test sets; BPID has no official split, so this report made a deterministic 70 / 10 / 20 one. Comparisons with the literature are between different test subsets and protocols: indicative only, never proof of superiority.
  • The Magellan splits themselves are not clean: identical pairs appear in train and test (96 on Amazon-Google, 16 on Abt-Buy), and 18, 1 and 13 identical pairs carry contradictory labels on Amazon-Google, Walmart-Amazon and Abt-Buy.
  • Sections A to C use samples of about 600 test pairs; the few-shot and best-stack sections use about 1 000. Read the printed confidence intervals, not the point estimates.
  • FEBRL4 is synthetic and one-to-one. Real contact data has clusters larger than two records; how to group them (verified merge or centre clustering instead of connected components) is not tested yet.
  • The classifier is scikit-learn code written for this bench and is stronger than the published Magellan numbers; it stands in for Zingg's classifier, not for Zingg's blocking.
  • Zingg ran on default settings with 6 to 12 active-learning rounds and no sweep of its sampling, partitioning, field types or thresholds. An expert might configure it better, and these runs do not establish a structural ceiling.
  • Nothing here has touched the lake's real people data. Sending it to Jev is still an open decision recorded in goals/goal_mdm.md.
